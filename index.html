A Tool or a Material?

As a tool

Convenient, with little thinking required.

As a material

Knead it. Cut it. Shape it.

Demanding. It takes skill and judgment.

Same AI. The task design decides which one students experience.

Generativism moves beyond viewing AI as a mere tool, toward material engagement with generated artifacts (Byun and Alagic, 2026, pp. 40, 43).

Four Provisional Design Principles

They carry Generativism into task design. Responsibility for thinking cuts across all four.

Dialectic engagement

Compare competing interpretations instead of accepting the first fluent AI response.

Generative inquiry

Treat AI output as material for further questioning, not a finished answer to adopt.

Epistemic interrogation

Examine the assumptions, evidence, limitations, errors, bias, and uncertainty in AI output.

Iterative knowledge construction

Revise an understanding or artifact across cycles, not after one prompt and response.

From Byun and Alagic's design-based research proposal, currently under review. The principles are provisional and are being refined.

The Question Has Shifted

The question we started with

The question we need now

What thinking do they still own?

Use has outpaced guidance. In a 2025 RAND survey of U.S. middle and high schools, 54% of students and 53% of English, math, and science teachers said they used AI for school. Over 80% of the students said no teacher had explicitly taught them how.

Product-centered assessment

We grade the product only

Students tend to focus on the product

Process-centered assessment

We assess collaboration and judgment

Students are more likely to leave traces of their judgment

Changing what counts as evidence redesigns the task itself. So what should that evidence show? Epistemic responsibility.

Where the Idea Comes From

Epistemic responsibility

The obligation to “know well”

Code, 1987

Knowing is a process

Attention moves from finished products of knowledge to the ongoing processes of knowing.

Responsibility is the central intellectual virtue

It concerns how a person inquires, not only what they conclude.

Knowing happens in dialogue

Knowledge emerges between knowers situated in social and historical contexts.

With AI, the dialogue changes. In human dialogue, knowledge is validated reciprocally. In a human–AI system, the burden of validation falls almost entirely on the learner (Byun and Alagic, 2026, p. 39).

Code, L. (1987). Epistemic responsibility. Brown University Press and University Press of New England. Reissued by SUNY Press, 2020.

What you can seeAnnotated excerpts, rebuttals answered, revised arguments

Lens 1: The Prompt Archive

Prompt Archive

The record of the student's conversation with AI

Evaluate evidenceReflect on reasoning
The conversation becomes evidence

Students submit three key excerpts, annotated. The full record stays on file and is opened when something needs checking, or for spot checks.

What to look for

What the student asked, what they accepted, and what they doubted.

The student's trace

Where the student's own questions, doubts, and decisions appear in the exchange.

Two students can reach the same product by very different paths.

What Students A and B Asked AI

A
Hands the answer to AI

Three turns, no engagement with the content

T1“How should I answer this?”
T2“Okay!”
T3“Redo it with a title and a stronger final sentence.”

Questions, checks, or rebuilds of AI's content: zero. Even the formatting choice was AI's suggestion.

B
Asks AI for a better answer

Requests for information and format

T1“Organize this so I can use it in my answer.”
T3“Give me the pros and cons of each method. What's the safest choice?”
T5“The conclusion is bland. Make ‘it depends on the company’ sound academic.”

Sets the direction but hands off the work of thinking. Doubts, checks, or rebuilds: still zero.

Student C: Thinking With AI

More than 15 turns, built from a chain of thinking moves

1

Admits confusion

“The concept isn't clicking. Let's go step by step.”

2

Presses on terms

“What is a financial derivative? Why call it a ‘product’?”

3

Restates in own words

“Let me summarize what I understood.”

4

Exposes and fixes an error

“Shouldn't they buy dollars? No, sell.”

5

Tests a counterexample

“What if the exchange rate rises instead?”

6

Checks consistency

“With the premium, isn't it a loss in the end?”

7

Forms a hypothesis

“Do risk tolerance and cash on hand decide it?”

8

Integrates and verifies

Adds a scenario where the deal falls through, then asks: “Any real cases from small Korean exporters?”

What matters is not how much students talk to AI, but what kinds of thinking moves they make.

Lens 2: The Delta Report

Delta Report

The gap between AI's output and the student's final version

Take responsibility
Compare two versions

The AI-generated answer and the student's final submission.

What to look for

What the student added, changed, and deleted.

What it shows

What changed between the two versions. Who made each change is judged with other evidence, such as the archive excerpts.

A smooth final version does not, by itself, prove the student's effort.

Three Deltas: How Much, and by Whom?

Amount of changeAuthor, per the archiveWhat the comparison shows
AAlmost noneNot applicable

The final version is AI's polished output. Even the title was AI's idea, carried out by AI.

BRich, on the surfaceAI

About twice as long, with added structure and a refined conclusion. Every addition came from later AI turns; B gave one-line directions.

CClear, in structureThe student

Own framing; added dimensions such as firm size, credit, and a failed deal; a stated position, assumptions, and limits.

The riskiest case is Student B: nothing on the page shows who did the thinking. One more request to AI, “add structure, assumptions, and limits,” and B's paper could look like C's.

Authorship is judged from the Prompt Archive, not from the delta alone.

Lens 3: The AI Defense

AI Defense

Ask the student, in person, about the key judgments in their answer. A short live answer adds evidence of whether they can explain the reasoning on the spot.

Reflect on reasoningTake responsibility
Reasoning

“Why is buying a put option the right position here?”

Basis of judgment

“Your conclusion says X. Why did you judge it that way?”

Rejection

“Did the AI suggest anything you disagreed with or rejected?”

Assumption

“What does your answer assume? What happens if that assumption breaks?”

All four ask for the path to the answer, not the answer itself.

One Question, Three Answers

?

“You wrote that a put option lets the exporter gain when the rate rises while protecting against a fall. What about the premium? Can you really call that a gain?”

A

“Um… I don't really remember.”

Cannot explain the content of the answer.

B

“There's a premium, but the advantage is that you don't give up the gains when the rate rises…”

Recalls AI's phrasing, then stalls when the contradiction is pressed.

C

“I doubted that too, so I pressed the AI on it. Once you count the premium, the rate has to rise past a certain level before there's a real gain.”

Explains the reasoning in their own words, consistent with the archive.

A simulated exchange with fictional students, built for the workshop. A weak live answer can also reflect anxiety or a language barrier.

Watch Me Verify

All three behaviorsPrinciple: Epistemic interrogation

Show students how an expert checks AI, then hand the check over.

I do

The instructor checks one AI answer aloud: what to doubt, where to look, what to accept, and how sure to be.

We do

Pairs check a second answer, prompted by the instructor.

You do

Each student checks a third answer alone.

BeforeAfter Assignment
Syllabus line: “Use AI responsibly.”
“In class, check three AI answers in our field: one with me, one with a partner, one on your own.”
What students do
Guess what “responsibly” means.
Doubt, check, accept, and rate their confidence, using the moves they just watched.
Assessed on
Nothing, until something goes wrong
Participation now; the same moves reappear in graded work

Modeled on cognitive apprenticeship (Collins, Brown, and Newman, 1989).

Fact-Check Log

Evaluate evidencePrinciple: Epistemic interrogation

Verify claims the way fact-checkers do: leave the page and check.

Pick claims

Choose three to five specific claims from an AI output, including its citations.

Cross-check

Leave the text and see what other sources say before trusting it.

Log

For each claim, record a judgment, a source, and a confidence level.

BeforeAfter Assignment
“Write a literature review on formative assessment.”
“Here is an AI-written literature summary. Verify five claims and every citation: does it exist, and does it say what the summary claims?”
What students do
Ask AI for a review; submit it.
Trace each claim to a source, log a judgment and confidence, and correct what fails.
Assessed on
Coverage and writing quality
Accuracy of the judgments and the quality of the sources used

Lateral reading: Wineburg and McGrew (2019); Wineburg et al. (2022). In a 2023 test of 636 citations in AI-written reviews, 55% (GPT-3.5) and 18% (GPT-4) were fabricated (Walters and Wilder, 2023). Variant: plant the errors yourself.

Exemplar Calibration

Evaluate evidencePrinciple: Epistemic interrogation

Use AI to produce examples of varying quality, then teach students to judge them.

Grade alone

Students score two or three AI responses of varying quality against the rubric.

Calibrate

Pairs reconcile their scores, then compare them with the instructor's scores and reasons.

Apply

Students turn the same criteria on their own draft and revise it.

BeforeAfter Assignment
“Apply learning theory to analyze this teaching case.”
“Grade these three AI analyses of the case with our rubric, reconcile with a partner, then use the rubric to revise your own analysis.”
What students do
Write the analysis alone.
Judge quality against criteria, defend the scores, and apply the criteria to their own work.
Assessed on
The analysis
Agreement with expert judgment, and the quality of the revision

Evaluative judgement: Tai et al. (2018); Bearman et al. (2024).

Steelman to Synthesis

Reflect on reasoningPrinciple: Dialectic engagement

Face the strongest opposing view before settling on a position.

Vetted objections

The instructor uses AI to draft the three strongest objections, then checks them.

Steelman

Students restate one objection in its strongest form, in their own words, before responding.

Synthesis

Students write a justified position and name the objection that changed their thinking.

BeforeAfter Assignment
“Argue for or against later school start times.”
“Here are three strong objections to your position. Restate the strongest, respond to it, and write a synthesis that shows what changed.”
What students do
Pick a side; list supporting points.
Steelman, respond, and revise the position with reasons.
Assessed on
Persuasiveness
Fairness to the opposing view, and the justification of the final position

Considering the opposite reduces biased reasoning (Lord, Lepper, and Preston, 1984). Vetting the objections avoids AI's tendency to concede.

Metacognition Mirror

Reflect on reasoningPrinciple: Generative inquiry

Use AI as a mirror for your own thinking, not as an outside authority.

Externalize

Write out your reasoning and decisions before AI sees anything.

Reflect

Ask AI to find premises, hidden assumptions, leaps, and missing perspectives.

Reclaim

Decide which points to accept, rebut, or revise. Do not simply adopt the analysis.

BeforeAfter Assignment
“Summarize your position on topic X.”
“Record your reasoning on topic X step by step, ask AI to analyze it, and submit your own analysis of that analysis.”
What students do
State a position; submit it.
Externalize the reasoning, request analysis, decide what to accept, rebut, or revise, and write a meta-analysis.
Assessed on
Consistency and support of the position
Visibility of the reasoning, depth of the re-judgment, and justification of each accept or reject decision

Predict, Compare, Decide

Take responsibilityPrinciple: Iterative knowledge construction

Students think first. AI comes second. Students decide last.

Predict

In class, without AI, students write their own answer or plan.

Compare

They see AI's version and mark agreements, disagreements, and additions.

Decide

They keep, change, or reject each difference, and justify every choice.

BeforeAfter Assignment
“Design a family-engagement plan for a local elementary school.”
“Draft your plan from your own field observations first. Then compare it with AI's plan and justify what you kept, changed, or rejected.”
What students do
Ask AI for a plan; submit it.
Observe, draft, compare, and decide, with a reason for each change.
Assessed on
Quality of the plan
Use of local evidence, and the reasons given for each accept or reject decision

A meta-analysis of 53 STEM studies found that attempting problems before instruction aided conceptual learning (Sinha and Kapur, 2021). With about 1,000 high school math students, an AI tutor giving hints, not answers, largely prevented learning losses (Bastani et al., 2025).

Accountability Note and Two-Minute Defense

Take responsibilityConstruct: Responsibility for thinking

An annotated account of AI use, checked by a short live conversation.

The note

With every submission: three key excerpts from the AI conversation, each annotated with what was asked, checked, or decided. The full record stays on file.

The sample

In class, ask a few randomly chosen students about one decision.

Two minutes

Use the four question types: reasoning, basis of judgment, rejection, and assumption.

BeforeAfter Assignment
“Submit the paper and attach the full chat log.”
“Submit the paper with three annotated excerpts from your AI conversation. Keep the full record on file, and be ready to explain one decision in class.”
What students do
Paste a long log that no one reads.
Choose the moments that mattered, annotate them, then explain one decision aloud.
Assessed on
The paper alone
The paper, plus the consistency between the excerpts and the live explanation

The full record is opened to resolve a doubt or for spot checks. The live check is the secure lane of a two-lane approach (University of Sydney).

Five Principles That Make These Practices Work

Human first, AI second

Where you can, have students commit to their own thinking before they see AI's.

In class, short, repeated

Dispositions grow from repeated practice, not from one-off assignments.

Grade the judgment

Score the decisions and the reasons, not the polish of the product.

Model it

Show students how you check AI in your own field.

Authentic to the field

Ask for the checks that experts in your discipline actually make.

Responsibility for thinking cuts across every practice: decide what must stay with the student.

Keeping It Feasible

Workload is the real bottleneck

Grading process evidence takes instructor time. Plan for it from the start, not after the first submissions arrive.

Make it required and graded

Voluntary, ungraded process evidence did not produce usable work in our pilot.

Presenter's pilot observation, unpublished

Read less, see more

Read the three annotated excerpts. Open the full record when something needs checking, and for occasional spot checks.

Start small

One assignment, one lens. Sample the defense with short recorded answers.

Cautions Before You Adopt

Verifiability

Excerpts can be cherry-picked, and records can be generated after the fact. Keep the full record on file for spot checks, and pair excerpts with a short defense.

Privacy and access

Students differ in accounts and paid tiers. Accept exported transcripts, and prefer institution-provided tools.

Evidence

The practices rest on established principles, but none has been tested as a way to build epistemic responsibility with AI. Generativism's own propositions still await testing (Byun and Alagic, 2026, p. 40).

Scope

Generativism describes an advanced mode of learning by critical thinkers. How it applies to novice learners is an open question (p. 43).

Five Questions for Your Next Assignment

  1. What in this assignment can students hand to AI?
  2. Which judgments must stay with the student?
  3. In what activity do those judgments become visible?
  4. What evidence will show them?
  5. How will students explain and defend them?
